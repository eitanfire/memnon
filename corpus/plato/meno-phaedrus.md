## Passage: Virtue Cannot Be Taught?
Meno: Can you tell me, Socrates — is virtue something that can be taught? Or does it come by practice? Or is it neither teaching nor practice that gives it to a man but natural aptitude or some other thing?
Socrates: I don't know whether virtue can be taught or not. I don't even know what virtue is.

*Meno, 70a*

## Passage: Learning as Recollection
The soul, since it is immortal and has been born many times, has seen all things both here and in the other world, and has learned everything that is. So we need not be surprised if it can recall the knowledge of virtue or anything else which it had before.

*Meno, 81c–d*

## Passage: Inquiry and the Paradox
You say that a man cannot inquire about what he knows, for he knows it; nor yet about what he does not know, for he does not know what to inquire about. Socrates replies: I do not insist that my account is right in every detail, but I would argue that we will be better and braver and less helpless if we believe that one ought to seek.

*Meno, 80d–86b (paraphrase)*

## Passage: The Madness of Love (Phaedrus)
The greatest blessings come by way of madness, indeed of madness that is heaven-sent. It was when they were mad that the prophetess at Delphi and the priestesses at Dodona achieved so much for which both states and individuals in Greece are thankful.

*Phaedrus, 244a–b*

## Passage: The Soul as Charioteer
The soul is like a pair of winged horses and a charioteer. One horse is noble and good, and the other is its opposite. The task of our charioteer is difficult and troublesome.

*Phaedrus, 246a–b*

## Passage: On Writing and Memory
Writing is unfortunately like painting; the products of painting look like living beings, but if you ask them a question they maintain a solemn silence. And when it is ill-treated or unjustly abused it always needs its parent to come to its rescue; it is quite incapable of defending or helping itself.

*Phaedrus, 275d*
