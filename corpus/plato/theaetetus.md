## Passage: Socratic Midwifery
My art of midwifery is in general like theirs; the only difference is that my patients are men, not women, and my concern is not with the body but with the soul that is in travail of birth. And the highest point of my art is the power to prove by every test whether the offspring of a young man's thought is a false phantom or instinct with life and truth.

*Theaetetus, 150b–c (trans. Cornford)*

## Passage: The Barrenness That Drives Inquiry
Those who associate with me are like women in their first pregnancy — they are full of things they do not understand, and they are in pain because they cannot bring them to birth. I help them to bring forth what they are carrying. And the result is that many of them who are apparently dull, when they associate with me, make progress in a surprising way.

*Theaetetus, 151a–b*

## Passage: The Question of Knowledge
Socrates: What is knowledge? Theaetetus: I don't know how to answer that question. Socrates: But that is the very question — not what things are there knowledge of, but what is knowledge itself?

*Theaetetus, 146e*

## Passage: Protagoras — Man Is the Measure
Protagoras says that man is the measure of all things — of what is, that it is; of what is not, that it is not. Perception then is always of what is, and as knowledge it is infallible.

*Theaetetus, 152a (presenting the view for examination)*

## Passage: The Wax Tablet
Imagine that our minds contain a block of wax, which in this or that individual may be larger or smaller, and composed of wax that is comparatively pure or muddy, and harder in some, softer in others, and sometimes of just the right consistency. Let us call it the gift of the Muses' mother, Memory, and say that whenever we wish to remember something we hear or conceive in our minds, we hold this wax under our perceptions and thoughts and take an imprint from them, as we might take impressions from signet rings.

*Theaetetus, 191c–d*

## Passage: The Aviary
Imagine a mind that contains an aviary stocked with birds of every kind. When we are children this enclosure is empty; birds represent pieces of knowledge. Acquiring knowledge is like catching birds and putting them in the aviary. But possessing knowledge and having it in hand are different things. A man may possess a bird without holding it at the moment.

*Theaetetus, 197d–e*

## Passage: The Value of Aporia
The result of our conversation is that I know absolutely nothing about the matter. But I have gained this much — if ever I have a child of the mind, I shall not be over-hasty in acknowledging it as mine before I have submitted it to your scrutiny and found that it is worthy of rearing.

*Theaetetus, 210b–c*
